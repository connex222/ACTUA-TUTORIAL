    Lesson 1 of 10

    What a bank feed is, and isn’t

    3 minutes

    In this lesson
    • What a bank feed does
    • Why a feed alone doesn’t make your books right

    A bank feed is a secure link between a business bank account and its accounting software. Each day it copies the bank’s transactions into the software, so nobody has to type them in.

    What it does

    Brings in the date, description and amount of every cleared transaction.

    What it doesn’t know

    What the money was for. A £48 payment could be a phone bill, a gift or a mistake.

    Your job

    Tell the software what each line is, then prove the totals agree with the bank.

    So there are two separate jobs, and this course covers both:

    1. Reviewing each bank line: saying what it is (lessons 3 to 5).
    2. Reconciling: proving the software’s balance matches the bank statement on a set date (lessons 6 to 9).
    Pending transactions

    Feeds usually bring in only transactions that have cleared. A card payment still “pending” at the bank won’t appear until it clears.

    Lesson 2 of 10

    How bank feeds connect

    4 minutes

    In this lesson
    • How a feed is set up safely
    • What to do when there’s no feed

    Setting up a feed is similar in every package:

    1. In the software’s banking area, choose to connect or link a bank account.
    2. Pick the bank from the list.
    3. You’re sent to the bank’s own secure page. Sign in there and give permission. The accounting software never sees the banking password.
    4. Back in the software, choose which account to link and how far back to bring in transactions.
    Permission runs out

    Banks ask you to renew permission from time to time. When a feed quietly stops bringing in new lines, an expired permission is the most likely reason. Check feeds are up to date before any VAT return or month end.

    No feed? Import a file

    If a bank can’t connect, download a statement file from online banking (usually a CSV file) and import it into the software. Watch the dates: importing a period the feed has already brought in creates duplicates.

    Start date matters

    When a feed is first connected, check it starts the day after the last transaction already in the books. An overlap is the classic cause of duplicate lines.

    Lesson 3 of 10

    The golden rule: match before you create

    5 minutes

    In this lesson
    • The three things you can do with a bank line
    • Why matching prevents double counting

    Every bank line needs one of three decisions. Each package uses slightly different words, but the idea is identical.

    DecisionUse it whenCalled
    MatchThe transaction is already in the books, such as an invoice the customer has now paid, or a bill you’ve now paid.Match / OK (Xero) / Match (QuickBooks, Sage)
    CreateIt’s not in the books yet, such as a card purchase or a bank charge.Create (Xero) / Add (QuickBooks) / create a transaction (Sage)
    ExcludeIt shouldn’t be in the books at all, usually because it’s a duplicate.Exclude (QuickBooks) / Delete or remove the line (Xero, Sage)
    Why “match first” matters

    When you raise an invoice, the sale is already recorded. If the customer’s payment is then created as new income instead of matched, the same sale is counted twice, profit is overstated, too much tax may be paid, and the invoice still shows as unpaid. Always look for an existing record before creating anything.

    A good routine for every line

    1. Read the description and amount.
    2. Ask: is there already an invoice, bill, payment or transfer for this? If yes, match.
    3. If not, ask: is it real business money? If yes, create it with the right category and VAT.
    4. If it’s a duplicate of a line you’ve already dealt with, exclude it.
    5. If you’re not sure, leave it and ask. An unreviewed line is safer than a wrong one.

    Lesson 4 of 10

    Dealing with tricky bank lines

    6 minutes

    In this lesson
    • The seven kinds of line that cause most mistakes
    • What to do with each
    A customer pays an invoice

    Match it to the invoice. Never create it as new sales. If one payment covers several invoices, match it to all of them together.

    You pay a supplier’s bill

    Match it to the bill you entered earlier. Creating it again would count the cost twice.

    Money moves between two of the business’s own accounts

    This is a transfer, not income or spending. Record it as a transfer between the two bank accounts. It will then appear in both feeds and match on each side.

    Card machine, PayPal or Stripe payouts

    These payouts usually arrive with fees already taken off. The sale was bigger than the money received. The full sale needs recording as income and the fee as a cost, so split the line or use the payment provider’s own feed or app connection. Ask your accountant how your client’s file is set up.

    The owner pays for something personal from the business account

    Don’t exclude it; it’s real money that left the account. It’s recorded to a special category: drawings for a sole trader or partnership, or the director’s loan account for a limited company. Check which with your accountant.

    A refund comes in

    A refund from a supplier reduces a cost; it isn’t sales. Match it to the supplier credit note if one exists, or create it against the original cost category.

    One payment covers several things

    Use split to divide one bank line across several categories, such as a supermarket receipt that includes both office supplies and staff refreshments.

    VAT needs paperwork

    When you create a cost with VAT, there must be a valid VAT invoice or receipt behind it. Attach it to the transaction where the software allows.

    Lesson 5 of 10

    Bank rules, used safely

    4 minutes

    In this lesson
    • What makes a good rule
    • How rules go wrong

    A bank rule tells the software how to handle a line it sees regularly. For example: “If the description contains BT GROUP, code it to Telephone, supplier BT, 20% VAT.” Sage, Xero and QuickBooks all have rules.

    Good rulesRisky rules
    Same supplier, same purpose every time (phone, rent, insurance)Suppliers that sell many things (Amazon, supermarkets)
    Precise conditions (“contains BT GROUP”)Loose conditions (“contains BT” also catches “BTL Supplies”)
    Rules that suggest, so a person still checksRules that post automatically with nobody looking
    Rules never apply to customer payments

    A rule that codes every receipt from a customer to “Sales” will create income instead of matching invoices. That double-counts sales. Leave customer and supplier payments to matching.

    Review rules once a year

    Suppliers change, VAT rates change, and old rules carry on regardless. A quick yearly check keeps them honest.

    Lesson 6 of 10

    What reconciling proves

    3 minutes

    In this lesson
    • The one number reconciliation checks
    • Why it’s more than reviewing the feed

    A bank reconciliation compares the balance in the software with the closing balance on the real bank statement, on the same date. When every transaction is accounted for, the difference is £0.00.

    Reviewing the feed and reconciling aren’t the same. A feed can be fully reviewed and still be wrong, because:

    • a line was deleted or excluded by mistake;
    • a transaction was entered by hand and also brought in by the feed;
    • the feed missed some days, or brought in the same days twice;
    • someone edited an old transaction that had already been checked.

    Only comparing against the bank’s own statement catches all of these.

    Why accountants insist on it

    VAT returns, management accounts and year-end accounts are only reliable if the bank is reconciled first. It’s the foundation everything else is built on.

    Lesson 7 of 10

    How to reconcile, in any software

    5 minutes

    In this lesson
    • The universal reconciliation method
    • What to have ready before you start

    Before you start

    • The bank statement for the period, as a PDF or paper copy.
    • Every feed line for the period reviewed (lessons 3 to 5).
    • The last reconciliation finished. You can’t skip a month.

    The method

    1. Open the reconciliation screen for the bank account.
    2. Check the opening balance agrees with the opening balance on the statement. If it doesn’t, stop: an earlier period has a problem (see “Something’s gone wrong”).
    3. Enter the statement’s end date and closing balance exactly as printed.
    4. Tick each transaction in the software that appears on the statement. Leave unticked anything that hasn’t reached the bank yet.
    5. Add anything on the statement that’s missing from the software, such as bank charges or interest.
    6. When the difference is £0.00, finish the reconciliation. Never finish with a difference.
    7. Keep a copy of the reconciliation report with the statement.
    Don’t change reconciled transactions

    Once a transaction is reconciled, editing or deleting it breaks every reconciliation after it. If something reconciled needs correcting, speak to your accountant first.

    Xero works slightly differently

    In Xero, each bank statement line is “reconciled” as soon as you match or create it and select OK. In Xero’s normal workflow there’s no separate tick-off step. That makes the final check, comparing Xero’s balance with the real statement in the Bank Reconciliation report, even more important.

    Lesson 9 of 10

    Finding a difference

    5 minutes

    In this lesson
    • Check the obvious things first
    • Two detective tricks that use the difference itself

    Check these first

    1. Is the closing balance typed exactly as on the statement? Is the end date right?
    2. Does the opening balance agree with the statement? If not, the problem is in an earlier period.
    3. Is there a line on the statement you haven’t ticked, or that’s missing from the software?
    4. Is anything ticked that isn’t on the statement?
    5. Is there a duplicate: same date, same amount, twice?

    Two detective tricks

    Divides exactly by 9?

    Two digits were probably swapped when typed, such as £54 entered as £45 (difference £9). Look for a transaction with swapped digits.

    Halve the difference

    If a payment was entered as money in instead of money out, the difference is twice the amount. A £96 difference? Look for a £48 transaction going the wrong way.

    The difference is the amount

    A difference equal to one transaction usually means that transaction is missing, unticked or duplicated.

    Never force it

    Never post a “balancing figure” to make the difference disappear. It hides a real error that will cause problems later. Save your progress and ask your accountant.

    Good habits

    3 minutes

    In this lesson
    • A simple weekly and monthly routine
    How oftenWhat to do
    WeeklyReview all new feed lines while they’re fresh in your memory. Chase anything you don’t recognise.
    MonthlyReconcile every bank, credit card and loan account to the statement. Keep the report.
    Before each VAT returnCheck feeds are up to date and every account is reconciled to the end of the VAT period.
    YearlyReview bank rules, and make sure every account is reconciled to the year-end date for your accountant.
    Little and often wins

    Twenty minutes a week is far easier than a whole day at the end of the quarter, when nobody can remember what a £37.50 card payment was for.

        Something’s gone wrong

        Select a problem to see the fix.

        The feed has stopped bringing in new lines

        The bank permission has most likely expired. Look for a reconnect or renew message on the bank account in your software and sign in to the bank again. Then check no days were missed between the last line and the new ones.

        Every transaction appears twice

        Usually a statement file was imported for days the feed had already brought in, or the feed was reconnected with an overlapping start date. Remove the duplicate lines before reviewing. If duplicates have already been processed, ask your accountant before deleting anything.

        The opening balance doesn’t agree with the statement

        Something in an earlier, already-reconciled period has changed: a reconciled transaction was edited or deleted, or a transaction was backdated. Don’t carry on. Sage, Xero and QuickBooks each have reports that show changes to reconciled items. Ask your accountant.

        The difference won’t reach £0.00

        Work through lesson 9: check the closing balance and date, look for missing or unticked lines, look for duplicates, then try the “divide by 9” and “halve it” tricks. Save your progress rather than finishing with a difference.

        A customer paid, but the invoice still shows as unpaid

        The payment was probably created as new income instead of matched. Undo or unreconcile that line, then match it to the invoice. If it’s in a period already reported for VAT, ask your accountant first.

        Reference

        Words explained

        Bank feed
        A secure link that copies bank transactions into accounting software.
        Bank line
        One transaction from the bank, also called a statement line.
        Bank rule
        An instruction for handling lines that repeat.
        Closing balance
        The balance at the end of a bank statement.
        Create
        Record a bank line as a new transaction because nothing exists for it yet.
        Difference
        The gap between the statement balance and the reconciled balance. It must be £0.00.
        Director’s loan account
        Where a limited company records money taken out by, or owed to, a director.
        Drawings
        Money a sole trader or partner takes out of the business for personal use.
        Exclude
        Remove a bank line, usually a duplicate, so it never reaches the books.
        Match
        Link a bank line to a record already in the books.
        Opening balance
        The balance at the start of a bank statement.
        Reconcile
        Prove the books agree with the bank statement on a set date.
        Split
        Divide one bank line across several categories.
        Transfer
        Money moving between two of the business’s own accounts.
