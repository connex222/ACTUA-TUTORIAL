    Lesson 1 of 7

    Why anti-money laundering matters

    4 minutes

    In this lesson
    • The law behind it
    • Who supervises the practice

    Accountants and bookkeepers are in the regulated sector. Criminals use businesses and professional advisers to make dirty money look clean, so the law puts duties on practices to spot and report it.

    LawWhat it requires
    Money Laundering Regulations 2017 (amended, most recently in 2026)Risk assessment, customer due diligence, policies, training and record keeping
    Proceeds of Crime Act 2002Offences of money laundering, failing to report suspicion, and tipping off
    Terrorism Act 2000Similar duties for terrorist financing
    These are personal duties

    Some offences apply to you personally, not just the practice. Failing to report a suspicion in the regulated sector can be a criminal offence.

    Supervision is changing

    Every practice must be supervised for AML, currently by a professional body or HMRC. The government has confirmed that the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) will become the single AML supervisor for accountancy and other professional services. Legislation is going through Parliament; the practice will tell you about any changes.

    Risk assessment

    4 minutes

    In this lesson
    • Firm-wide and client risk

    The practice must have a written firm-wide risk assessment, and each client must be risk-assessed before work starts and kept under review.

    Risk factorHigher risk examples
    ClientComplex ownership, reluctance to give information, politically exposed persons
    ServiceHandling client money, company formation, payroll for cash businesses
    GeographyLinks to high-risk countries
    DeliveryNever meeting the client, unusual instructions through third parties
    SectorCash-intensive businesses, property, high-value goods
    Low, medium or high

    The rating decides the level of checks: simplified, standard or enhanced due diligence. Record the reasons for the rating.

    Lesson 3 of 7

    Customer due diligence

    6 minutes

    In this lesson
    • Identify and verify
    • When enhanced checks apply
    1. Identify the client: full name, date of birth, address; for companies, registered details.
    2. Verify it from reliable, independent sources, such as photo ID, electronic checks or certified digital identity services.
    3. Identify the beneficial owners (usually anyone owning or controlling more than 25%) and take reasonable steps to verify them.
    4. Understand the purpose and nature of the relationship and, where needed, the source of funds.
    5. Complete this before starting work, and record it.
    SituationEnhanced due diligence
    Politically exposed person (PEP), family member or close associateYes; domestic PEPs start from a lower risk than foreign PEPs
    Links to a high-risk third countryYes, proportionate to the risk
    Unusually complex or large transactions with no clear purposeYes
    Anything rated high riskYes
    Ongoing monitoring

    Due diligence isn’t one-off. Keep details up to date and watch for activity that doesn’t fit what you know about the client. If you find a discrepancy between what you know and the Companies House PSC register, it must be reported to Companies House.

    Spotting red flags

    4 minutes

    In this lesson
    • Warning signs to watch for
    Red flagExample
    Unexplained wealth or cashA client with modest income suddenly buys property for cash
    Reluctance to provide informationDelays and excuses over ID or records
    Transactions with no business senseMoney moving through accounts with no clear purpose
    Unusual paymentsFees paid by an unconnected third party, or overpayments followed by refund requests
    Records that don’t add upTakings far higher than the business could plausibly generate
    Pressure or secrecyUrgency, or requests to avoid putting things in writing
    Suspicion is a low bar

    You don’t need proof. If something makes you suspect money laundering, it should be reported internally.

    Lesson 5 of 7

    Reporting suspicions, and tipping off

    5 minutes

    In this lesson
    • What to do
    • What never to do
    1. Report your suspicion promptly to the practice’s MLRO (Money Laundering Reporting Officer, also called the nominated officer), using the internal report form.
    2. Don’t investigate further or confront the client.
    3. The MLRO decides whether to make a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) to the National Crime Agency.
    4. If the work involves dealing with suspected criminal property, the MLRO may need to ask the NCA for a defence before the work continues.
    5. Keep the report confidential and separate from the client file.
    Tipping off is a crime

    Never tell the client, or anyone else, that a report has been or might be made, or that they’re under investigation. Doing so can be a criminal offence.

    Lesson 7 of 7

    What changed in 2026

    3 minutes

    In this lesson
    • The 2026 amendments

    The Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (Amendment) Regulations 2026 were made on 9 June 2026, and most changes came into force on 30 June 2026. They tune the existing rules rather than replacing them.

    ChangeWhat it means
    Revised enhanced due diligenceMore targeted, risk-based EDD requirements
    High-risk third countriesA more proportionate approach
    Off-the-shelf companiesDue diligence applies to any sale of a ready-made company
    Digital identityGovernment guidance on using certified digital identity services for checks
    Keep up to date

    The CCAB Anti-Money Laundering Guidance for the Accountancy Sector remains the main reference. Check for the revised edition and the practice’s updated policy.
